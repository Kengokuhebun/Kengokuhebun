When my daughter had her baby, our whole world sent flowers. I counted eleven arrangements in her kitchen.

I was there for the first week — I'm her mother, that's the job — sleeping in the guest room, running the dishwasher, learning where she keeps things and keeping my opinions about it to myself. Kate is thirty-one. The baby came in the middle of an October night, healthy and loud, and by that Friday the doorbell had become a florist's route stop.

I want to be careful here, because every one of those arrangements was love. People are good. The cards were kind. But I watched what eleven arrangements actually do to a household with a five-day-old in it, and somebody has to say it: flowers are a gift that assigns chores. Somebody has to find a vase, and the house has four. Somebody has to trim stems, change water, move them off the one counter where the bottle things live. That somebody was me, and I was glad to be useful, and also: flowers die in a week.

By day ten I carried three of them out to the compost while Kate slept. Petals all the way down the hall, like the end of a parade. Somewhere between the hallway and the compost pile, I started planning something that would not wilt. I already knew its shape, roughly: open on top, no lid, soft-sided, small enough to park beside a chair without becoming furniture — and needing exactly nothing from anybody. No vase, no water, no counter space.

And here is the other thing I watched. Visitors came for the baby. Of course they did — she's magnificent. They held her for an hour, said what you say, asked what the baby needed. Nobody asked what Kate needed. The baby, for the record, needed nothing. The baby has never once done a load of laundry. It's the woman who just did the hardest thing of her life who needs things, and she is invisible in her own living room, and she would never say so.

So I started a list on my phone, quietly, of what I saw. The water cup, always across the room from wherever she got pinned. Snacks she could eat with one hand — she was starving at strange hours and wouldn't say it. The phone charger that didn't reach the feeding chair. Hair ties. Lip balm. The hand cream she left in the hospital bag. Small things. Sightline things. The things nobody sends.

I'll tell you what I did not do. I did not lecture a single visitor. I did not announce anything, or post anything, or explain my position on flowers to people who meant well. What I decided, somewhere in that week, was that I wasn't going to give my daughter a present at all. I was going to set something up before I left — a station, stocked for her, parked where she actually sits — and leave it behind me like a grab bar or a night light. Not wrapped, not handed over, not waiting on a thank-you. Installed.

What I ordered, from the guest room, on night eight, was a basket — deep, soft, quilted cotton, the kind of thing that can sit beside a chair for a year and belong there.

It came two days later, while I was still there, and I stocked it at the kitchen table after she'd gone up to bed. The forty-ounce cup with the handle and the straw. A ten-foot charging cable. The almonds, the good granola bars, the dark chocolate that doesn't need to be shared. Hair ties. Lip balm. The nice hand cream. A paperback she will not read for eight months, which is not what the paperback is for — hope goes in the basket too. Not one item for the baby. That was on purpose. Everyone else has the baby covered.

It's called a Quilty, by Emmafy — and I set it down next to the feeding chair, where her free hand actually lives, and went to bed without saying anything.

At 3:41 that morning my phone lit up on the nightstand. Kate: "how did you know about the hair ties." That's the whole text. I slept very well.

Here's why the shape of the thing matters, and I didn't fully understand it until I watched her use it. A woman feeding a baby has one hand, sometimes half of one. A drawer needs two. A zipper needs two and an argument. Nothing on top to fight — no lid, no flap — means she can see everything and reach anything without moving the person sleeping on her. And it stays put. That was the entire point of the gesture: I didn't hand her one more thing to manage, I installed something — and installed things don't wander. Her husband, Ben, reports that in six weeks nobody has moved it an inch. The feeding chair is where she gets pinned, and the basket is already there when it happens.

I went home at the end of the week. The flowers were gone before the baby could hold her own head up. When I visited at six weeks, the basket was exactly where I'd set it down, beside the feeding chair, restocked — she does that part herself now — and the paperback had moved to the nightstand. Not read. Closer.

My friend Joan's daughter is due in November, and Joan is already booked to stay the first week, the way I did. She asked me what she should do with that week, braced for me to say something wise about casseroles. I told her: everyone is going to send flowers, and the flowers will die in a week, and somebody will have to deal with the vases. You'll be the one standing in that kitchen. Stay your week, watch what your girl reaches for, and set up her corner before you leave. And here's the part that made Joan laugh: mine was a buy one, get one free when I ordered, so the second basket is already sitting at my house with Joan's name on it. She'll carry it in with her suitcase in November and stock it herself, at the end of her week, once she knows. She's not getting out of this.

If someone you love is having a baby, hear me on this. The baby will be fine. The baby has a committee. Set up a station for the woman — her water, her snacks, the charger that reaches — and leave it where she sits.
