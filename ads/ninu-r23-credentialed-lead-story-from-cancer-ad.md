# R23: Credentialed-lead survival story, rebuilt for Ninu

Written 2026-11-05. Reference: a very long (about 3,400 words) first-person advertorial selling a black walnut supplement as a cancer cure. **That ad is deceptive and dangerous.** It claims a cure for stage 4 cancer, invents authorities and results, and tells readers conventional medicine is a cover-up. Nothing from its claims, people, numbers or product carries over. I took only the persuasion mechanics, and I rebuilt them on Ninu's own facts (angle: habit and delivery; desire: "I was never the problem, the method was"; avatar: the postpartum mom; buyer language from `ninu-core-copy-resource-breakthrough-advertising.md`).

## 3. The rebuild (primary text)

```
After four restarts, three bottles of oil, a shower cap and a very wet towel, I finally worked out why my scalp oil routine never stuck.

It wasn't me.

I'm not a hair expert. I'm a tired mom of one who has gotten into bed with wet hands more nights than I want to count.

It was a Sunday, 10pm. I was sitting on the edge of the tub with a towel around my shoulders and a bottle of castor oil I'd bought in March. It was the fourth time I'd called that night "the start."

I'd done everything people told me to do. Of course I had.

The castor oil. Then the rosemary. Then a shower cap, because someone in a thread said to wear one. Then a silk pillowcase. Then a reminder on my phone that went off at 10pm and only made me feel worse.

I'd do it four nights in a row. Then the baby would be up three times, and I'd get into bed with wet hands and skip it.

Wet hands. In bed. Again.

Then I'd skip a second night. The bottle would drift to the back of the shelf, and I'd tell myself I'd start over Sunday.

My husband stopped asking if I'd done it. That was worse than if he'd asked.

Everyone gave me the same advice. Do it every night. Be consistent. Nobody told me how.

I stayed up one night searching. Most of what I found was influencers, expensive bundles, and the same sentence over and over. Just be consistent.

So I stopped reading and ran a test.

A clean white towel over my shoulders. The same routine, exactly the way I always did it. Then I looked at three places.

My part. Barely damp.

My fingers. Slick.

The towel. Soaked.

The oil was going everywhere except where I wanted it.

My first fix was a different bottle with a pump. Same problem, different bottle. A dropper puts the oil on your hair. Fingers put it on your hands, your neck and the towel. Neither was getting it to my scalp.

It wasn't discipline. It was the method. It was like watering the leaves when you meant the roots.

So I wrote down what a fix would have to do.

Land on my scalp, along the part.

Work with one hand, because the other one usually has a baby on it.

Let me see how much I'm using.

Work with the oil I already owned.

I found a dispenser with hollow silicone tips. It's called Ninu. It did all four.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because nothing dramatic did.

Week one. It's oil on a scalp. No rush, no tingle. The difference was that I did it. By the end of the week I hadn't skipped a night. That had never happened.

Week three. The baby was up four times and I fell asleep with my shoes on. I missed a night. I did it the next night. That was the whole plan. There was nothing to restart.

Week five. It was automatic, the same as washing my face.

I was never the problem. The method was.
```

## 5. Length and where it fits

About 815 words. The reference is about four times longer, and its urgency comes from a life at stake. Ninu's is a routine, so this is trimmed to avoid attracting story-only readers (resource section 3.2).

Distinct from R22: R22 turns on a question she asks herself; this one turns on a test she runs and the reader can repeat. It's concept #10 in the diversity matrix. Run it after R16, with the other long-form tests, one at a time.
