## 1. The ideas that matter, and what each means for a Ninu ad

| # | Idea (chapter) | Schwartz says | Ninu reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mass desire (1) | "Copy cannot create desire for a product." It can only focus desire that already exists. A desire has three dimensions: urgency, staying power, scope. Every product touches several desires, but only one can lead. | The lead desire decides everything after it. Ours is not hair regrowth (we can't promise it). Section 2 sets out what it is. |
| 2 | Two products (1) | The physical product does not sell. The functional product (what it does for her) sells. Physical facts justify the price and document the claim. | "Hollow silicone tips, 8ml chamber, 1 to 4ml marks" are proof and mechanism, not the promise. The promise is what the routine becomes. |
| 3 | Awareness (2) | Five stages, most aware to unaware. The more aware the market, the less you say. Each stage needs a different headline, and a headline for one stage fails in another. | Our cold audience is problem-aware and solution-aware (she knows scalp oiling) but product-unaware. So the copy must introduce a product, not just a feeling. |
| 4 | Headline's job (2) | The headline's only job is to get the second sentence read. A fifth-stage identification headline "must pick out the product's logical prospects" and "reject as many people as it attracts." | A hook has to filter, not just attract. This is the centre of the buyer-versus-consumer question (section 3). |
| 5 | Sophistication (3) | Stage 1 direct claim. Stage 2 bigger claim. Stage 3 new mechanism ("the emphasis shifts from what the product does to HOW it works"). Stage 4 improve the mechanism. Stage 5 identification again. | Scalp-oil applicators already exist (roller balls). Our market is stage 3 to 4. The mechanism must be near the top and sold hard. |
| 6 | Verbalization (4) | 38 ways to strengthen the headline once you have the idea. No formula works twice (5). Copying structure from another market produces "Echo Ads." | Our swipes (lactation, quilts, Disney bags) borrowed structure from markets with very different desires. Section 4 covers what that costs. |
| 7 | Intensification (7) | Build desire with fresh angles: put the claim in action, put the reader in the scene, show how to test it, stretch benefits over time, bring in an audience, contrast the black side, show how easy it is, summarise, put the guarantee to work. | This is where the product-in-use, the test, and the guarantee-as-climax come from. |
| 8 | Identification (8) | People buy roles as well as function: character roles and achievement roles. You cannot force a role the product can't carry. | "The mom who does it every night" is the role. It needs a bridge she believes, which the habit story already provides. |
| 9 | Gradualization (9) | Belief is built in sequence. Open with something she fully accepts, then chain acceptances to the claim. "One fully-believed promise" outsells ten partly believed ones. Language of logic ("here's why", "turns out") carries conviction. | Our long stories already do this well (Yes, Yes, Yes about the wet hands). They then stop one step short: the claim and the offer arrive late and thin. |
| 10 | Redefinition (10) | Remove the objection before she voices it: simplify (it's easy), escalate (it matters more than she thought), reduce price (change what she compares it to). | Three objections to redefine: "another gadget I'll skip", "is it worth it", "$49.99 for oil I already have". |
| 11 | Mechanization (11) | She asks three things in order: "Tell me more." "Oh yeah? Who says so?" "How does it work?" Answer each when she asks. "Price cuts must be justified. There must be a reason for them." | The sale price needs a reason (the Early Black Friday date is the real one). The mechanism paragraph must sit right after the problem. |
| 12 | Concentration (12) | Destroy the alternatives, but "never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution to that weakness at the same time." Use bad / good / bad / good. | Attack the method (dropper, fingers, reminders), never a named brand. |
| 13 | Camouflage (13) | Borrow belief from the medium: its format, its phrasing, and two moods, Understatement and Deadly Sincerity (pointing out the flaws in your own offer so the benefits are believed). | The Notes and Reddit native format is textbook camouflage. Deadly Sincerity is the technique we have used least and the one best suited to a skeptical mom. |
| 14 | Final touches (14) | Verification (proof placed where she asks for it), reinforcement, interweaving, sensitivity (shift copy type when her interest shifts), momentum, mood. "A new structure is a new ad." | Proof is where we are thinnest. We can't invent proof, so we use demonstrations, tests and the guarantee. |

## 2. Where Ninu sits

**Mass desire (ch 1).** Candidates, scored on Schwartz's three dimensions (my scoring, not data):

| Desire | Urgency | Staying power | Scope | Can we promise it? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stop or reverse postpartum hair loss | very high | very high | very high | No. Medical and regrowth claims are off the table. |
| Feel like myself again, stop hiding my hairline | high | high | high | Yes, as identification. It is a feeling, not an outcome. |
| Keep up a scalp routine I already believe in | medium | medium | narrow: only women who oil or want to | Yes. This is the claim that has converted. |
| Less mess | low | low | wide but shallow | Yes, but weak on its own. |

**Reading.** The proven claim (consistency) sits on the narrowest and least urgent desire. The urgent desire (hair, mirror, photos) is the one buyers carry, and we can only touch it through identification. That is why the winning copy works emotionally and still converts at 0.7% of sessions: the feeling is big and the promise is small. Neither is wrong. The copy has to bridge the two on purpose.

**Awareness (ch 2).** Problem-aware: she knows hair is falling and that scalp oiling exists (she bought oil). Product-unaware: she doesn't know a delivery tool is the missing piece. Schwartz's instruction for this stage: name the desire, prove it can be done, show the mechanism is in your product.

**Sophistication (ch 3).** Stage 3 to 4. Direct claims and bigger claims have been used up ("oil for hair growth" is everywhere). What is new is how the oil gets to her skin. Mechanism leads, and the claim follows it.

**Beliefs we can't fight (ch 6, 9).** She believes oiling is worthwhile (she bought oil), that she is the problem when she skips, and that influencer claims are fake ("not influencer BS real mom experience"). The copy builds on the first, reframes the second, and respects the third.

## 3. Buyer language versus content-consumer language

Your hypothesis: ads are drawing people who enjoy the story and don't intend to buy. Here is how I'd test it before changing anything.

**Evidence that fits the hypothesis**

| Signal | Data (Nov 2-3) | Why it points that way |
|---|---|---|
| Hook3 link CTR 4.0%, link CPC £0.53, 50 clicks | about 36 to 48 sessions, at most 1 add-to-cart | Many clicks, almost no intent. |
| Hook1_OG link CTR 3.5% | 45 clicks, 1 purchase | Strong clicks; cost came from CPM (£83.83), not from the clicks. |
| Sung video v3: hook rate 36.8%, 37% still watching at the 25% mark | link CTR not yet seen | Entertainment holds attention. Whether it clicks is open. |
| Store, 14 days to Oct 31 | 1,708 social sessions, 12 add-to-carts (0.70%) | The leak is before add-to-cart. |

**Evidence against, or that the hypothesis isn't proven**
- Hook1_OG's one sale is 1 in 45 clicks (2.2%), three times the page's usual rate. The same story copy did bring a buyer.
- One ad (ID ending 920342) sent 1,005 sessions and 0 add-to-carts. That looks like bad clicks or bots, not readers who enjoyed a story.
- The page was rebuilt for mobile on about Oct 31 and has not had a fair sample yet. The page and the audience are tangled together.
- Sessions with no ad ID (222) show an 8% add-to-cart rate. If that is warm or direct traffic, the page can convert people who already want it, which backs your view. If it is your own testing, it means nothing. I can't tell which.

**My view:** partly right, and it's testable. High link CTR with low add-to-cart is the signature of curiosity clicks. But the page change and the audience change happened together, so I wouldn't conclude yet.

### The structural reasons our copy may attract consumers (my reading of the files)

1. **The buyer information starts late.** In R13 the offer begins at 89.8% of the text, in R14 at 85.8%. Meta cuts long primary text behind "See more," so many readers never reach a product, a price or a guarantee. The reader who wanted a story got one. The one who wanted a product never saw it.
2. **Our swipes come from markets with a far stronger desire.** TryVera's reader is watching her baby fall off a growth curve. That urgency carries 4,000 words. A mom who skips scalp oil has a smaller emergency, so the same long, emotional shape attracts people who relate to the exhaustion more than to the product.
3. **The identification is about being tired, not about oiling.** "Baby up three times, wet hands" picks out any exhausted mom. Schwartz asks the headline to reject as many as it attracts. The words "scalp oil" do the filtering, and in Hook3 they are at the very end.
4. **No disqualifying statement.** Nothing in the copy says who it isn't for.
5. **Proof is thin and the offer is a closing paragraph.** No demonstration, no test, no price reason, no objection handling.

What I take from this: the persuasion architecture (belief in sequence) is the best part of the copy and should not be touched. What is missing is everything that turns an accepted story into a decision: mechanism in use, objections, a test, a reason for the price, a statement of who it isn't for, and the guarantee at the end as the last thing she reads.

A note on process. Schwartz is critical of word-substitution (ch 5). Rewriting a competitor's structure with our nouns is how the swipes were made. The structure is useful; the content must come from Ninu's market. Every sentence in the R15 draft came from our own facts or Ad C, which is right. What carried over from the source ads that shouldn't is the length, which suits a more urgent desire than ours.

## 5. Buyer-language bank (all lines compliance-checked)

Use these as building blocks. Each is behaviour or delivery only.

**Mechanism in use (ch 7: put the claim in action, bring in the reader)**
- "You part your hair. You press the tips against your scalp along the part. The oil releases right there, on the skin."
- "One hand. About two minutes. It works with the oil you already own."
- "A clear chamber with 1 to 4ml markings, so you can see how much you're using."

**Concentration, bad then good (ch 12)**
- "A dropper puts the oil on your hair. The tips put it on your scalp."
- "Fingers put it on your hands, your neck and the towel. The tips put it on the part."
- "A reminder gets you to start. An easier routine gets you to keep going."

**Redefinition (ch 10)**
- Simplify: "It isn't another routine. It's the same oil, from a different bottle." (confirm the oil in the kit is compatible before using)
- Escalate: "The oil only counts when it reaches your scalp. Everything else is laundry."
- Price: "$49.99 for the dispenser, a month of oil and the bonnet. About $1.67 a night to start." (49.99 divided by 30; true arithmetic. Add refill pricing only if the subscription is live.)

**Price reason-why (ch 11)**
- "It's $49.99 until Nov 10 for our early Black Friday sale. On Nov 11 it goes to $62.49." (only if the price really changes)

**Test and guarantee (ch 7, 14)**
- "Use it tonight. If the oil isn't landing where I said it would, you have 30 days to get your money back."
- "Thirty nights is enough to find out whether you stick with it."

**Deadly Sincerity (ch 13)**
- "What it won't do: it won't make your oil work harder, and it won't do the routine for you."
- "It makes the routine easy enough that you actually do it."
- "If you don't want to oil your scalp, this isn't for you."

**Language of logic (ch 9)**
- "Here's why." "Turns out." "That's the reason." "Which means."

**Momentum (ch 14)**
- "Here's what changed." "Here's the part nobody told me." "And it only took one night."

**Hooks that filter (ch 2, 4 #33 and #34: address the people who can't buy, address the prospect directly)**
- "For moms who already own a scalp oil and keep skipping it"
- "If your scalp oil is at the back of the shelf, read this"
- "Not for you if you've never tried scalp oiling"

## 6. Pre-ship checklist (copy)

1. One lead desire, named in a sentence. Does every paragraph serve it?
2. Who does the hook exclude? If nobody, rewrite it.
3. The words "scalp oil" or "scalp" appear in the first line she sees.
4. The product is introduced before 60% of the text.
5. The mechanism is shown in use, in the order she'd do it.
6. At least one alternative is named by method (dropper, fingers) and answered by a contrast.
7. The three objections (another gadget, worth it, price) each have a line.
8. Any price change has a reason (a date, a real sale).
9. One test she can run herself, tied to the guarantee.
10. One sentence that says what it won't do.
11. The guarantee is the last factual thing before the call to action.
12. Every claim passes section 7.
13. Read it aloud. Where did you want to stop? That is the point where she stops (Schwartz, ch 14).

## 7. Compliance overlay (overrides every technique above)

Schwartz writes for 1960s mail order, where a claim could be pushed to "the outer limits of both legality and believability." Do not copy that.

- No regrowth, shedding-stopped, medical or study claims. Do not intensify desire by describing hair coming back.
- No invented customers, quotes, numbers, "thousands of moms", experts or "doctors recommend." Schwartz's testimonial and expert techniques (ch 7 #6, #7) are off unless the proof is real.
- First-person story is brand voice, not a testimonial. Do not present it as a named customer.
- Scarcity and deadlines only if real. The Nov 10 and $62.49 lines are true only if the price actually changes.
- The ebook is promised on the page but not delivered. Leave it out of copy until it is.
- The line "Hair doesn't absorb oil. Skin does." is stated as an absolute. My recollection (not re-checked here) is that some oils, coconut oil in particular, do penetrate the hair shaft, and a knowledgeable reader could object. Schwartz is clear that a violated belief can sink the whole ad (ch 9). Safer wording: "A dropper puts most of the oil on your hair. I wanted it on my scalp." I have not changed the control, because the master doc says one variable per round, but I'd test this line.
- Check the claim "Made in the USA" and "postpartum-formulated" before using either.
