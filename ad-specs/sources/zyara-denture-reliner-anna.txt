I got f*cked for the first time in 13 years. I'm 64, I'm a widow, and the whole time, the thing I was scared of wasn't him.

It was my bottom teeth.

Six months ago I'd have told you that part of my life was dead and buried. So let me tell you what changed, because it wasn't me.

My name is Anna. My husband Gary was sick for ten years before he passed. I took care of him right to the end, and by the last few years we were more nurse and patient than husband and wife. Nobody had looked at me like a woman since.

I wasn't looking for anybody. I was doing my taxes. This was February.

Walt volunteers at the library on Thursdays, helping seniors file. He's 67, a widower too. He caught a mistake on my form that would've cost me $400, and then he asked if I wanted to go get pie.

I said yes before I could think about it.

Then I thought about it the whole way home, because I've had full dentures since I was 58.

The top is fine. The bottom has never once in six years stayed where it's supposed to.

And I'd quit so much because of it.

The first summer I quit corn on the cob, because it would pull the whole plate loose. I told everybody I'd just gone off it.

Then steak. Then apples. Then ribs, which used to be my whole Fourth of July. Then anything with a crust. Popcorn went the night a hull got up under my plate. I spent the whole movie digging at it with my tongue like a dog with a bone.

Every cup of coffee tasted a little like the cream. I stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing a smell in your own house.

At my grandson's band concert I laughed at something my sister said. My plate clicked so loud the man in front of us turned around. After that I laughed with my hand over my mouth.

I lost 40 pounds after Gary passed. It wasn't grief and it wasn't a diet. It was soup.

I lost so much that my wedding ring started sliding off. So I wrapped Scotch tape around the inside of the band, and it still slid every time I did dishes.

I carried a tube of cream in my purse like other women carry lipstick. Every three hours, back to the bathroom. Smear it, press it, wipe off the pink stuff that oozed out the sides, wait, pray.

So when Walt asked me out, I didn't worry about what to wear. I worried about what to order.

Our first date was at the diner out on Route 9. He got the meatloaf. I got potato soup and told him I'd had a big lunch, which was a lie, I hadn't eaten since breakfast.

Halfway through, I felt the plate start to float. I redid the cream in the ladies' room with my purse balanced on the sink.

When I came back, he'd ordered us a slice of cherry pie with two forks.

I ate the cherries off the top and left the crust, because crust is the thing that rocks a plate loose. He noticed. He didn't say a word, he just slid the crust over to his side and said, "I'm a crust man anyway."

On our fourth date we went to the movies. He held the popcorn out to me the whole time, and the whole time I shook my head. At my car he leaned in to kiss me, and I gave him my cheek.

He smiled and said, "Next time, then."

I sat in my driveway and cried with the engine running.

By our sixth date things were heading where things head, even at our age. My sister Rhonda, who has no shame at all, said, "Anna. Go buy some."

So I drove to the gas station two towns over, because I was not about to run into somebody from church.

The girl at the register was maybe 19. She rang up the condoms and my denture cream together. Then she looked at me and said, "Big weekend?"

That Saturday Walt came over for dinner. I made a pot roast so soft you could cut it with a spoon. I did my cream twice before he got there and once more during dessert.

After, we sat on the couch with the TV on and neither of us watching it. He put his hand on my face and kissed me. A real kiss, the kind I'd honestly forgotten about.

And my bottom plate came up off my gum.

I felt it lift. He felt it too. He pulled back about an inch and said, real gentle, "Was that your teeth?"

Oh, God.

I said I was tired, because I couldn't say the truth. I said it was late. I walked him to the door with my lips pressed shut so nothing else could move.

That box from the gas station was still in my nightstand, unopened, sitting on top of Gary's reading glasses.

I sat on the edge of the bed and thought, well. That part of my life went in the ground with Gary, and nobody bothered to tell me.

Next morning I texted Walt that I wasn't ready. He wrote back, "Okay. I'm not going anywhere."

I didn't answer him.

And I want you to know I didn't just roll over. I'd tried everything.

More cream. Then the extra hold kind. Then the powder, which tasted like chalk. Then the strips, which held a little longer and then curled up at the edges like old wallpaper.

I paid my dentist for a reline. That one actually worked, for about four months. Then it was floating again and he said I'd need another one.

Then he told me the real fix was implants. Medicare doesn't cover them. Three to six thousand dollars a tooth.

I sat in his parking lot a long time after that.

So I quit. No more pie, no more Walt. Pot roast for one.

Two weeks later I was in the locker room after water aerobics at the Y. Tuesday class, mostly women my age and older.

Bev is 71 and she's the loud one. She was sitting on the bench in her towel eating a whole Granny Smith apple, crunching it like a horse.

I said, "Bev, I'd give anything to eat an apple like that."

She laughed with her mouth wide open. Then she reached in and popped her bottom plate out to show me. She had to work at it, it made a little suction sound coming loose.

"Top and bottom since I was 55," she said.

I just stared at her, because I'd never seen anybody my age eat like that.

She asked what I used. I listed it all. Cream, extra hold, powder, strips, the reline. She nodded at every one like she'd done them all herself, because she had.

Then she grabbed my hand and turned my ring around so she could see the tape.

"What's this?"

I said I'd lost weight and it got loose.

"So your finger got smaller and the ring didn't," she said. "Honey, that's your mouth."

She said when your teeth come out, the bone under your gums starts to shrink. It never stops. Her dentist told her the bottom shrinks about four times faster than the top, which is why it's always the bottom.

"Your plate was made to fit the jaw you had the day they made it," she said. "That jaw's gone. Now there's a gap under there, and it gets wider every year."

"Cream doesn't fill that gap. It sits on top of it and tries to stick. It's your tape, Anna. It gets wet and it slides."

She said spit washes cream out in about three hours. That's the bathroom trip. That's the purse.

"And while it's washing out, where do you think it goes? Out the sides. Into your coffee. Down your throat."

That's why my coffee tasted like it. That's why I was scrubbing pink paste off my gums every night with a washcloth.

"And the gap's still sitting there under all that goop," she said. "That's where your popcorn goes."

I'd never once thought about why food got under there. I figured I was just eating wrong.

"The extra hold is just more tape," she said. "The powder and the strips, tape. They all sit on top of the gap."

"The reline worked," I said.

"Because it was the only thing you tried that filled the gap. Then your gum kept shrinking, and the gap came back."

I said, "So what, I pay a dentist every four months for the rest of my life?"

She shook her head. She said I needed something that fills the gap like a reline does. Something that takes the shape of my gum now, not the shape it used to be. Nothing sticky in it for spit to wash out. And something I could redo myself at the sink when my gum changes again.

I said, "That's it? It's just a gap?"

"It's just a gap. You can't glue a gap shut. You have to fill it."

Then she told me what she uses. Zyara.

I said, "Bev, I'm not putting some internet goop in my mouth."

She said, "You put drugstore goop in it three times a day."

She had me there.

Day two was my granddaughter Kayla's birthday lunch at my daughter's house. Burgers on the grill. Corn on the cob.

I took an ear. Just to see.

Halfway through it, Kayla put down her fork and said, "Grandma. You're eating the corn."

The whole table looked at me, and my daughter's eyes filled right up.

I didn't go to the bathroom once that day. That night there was nothing to scrub off my gums. The cream tube rode around in my purse untouched until it went in the trash that Friday.

That same night I peeled the tape off Gary's ring. I took it off for the first time since 1985 and put it in the drawer with his reading glasses.

Then I called Walt.

He came over Saturday. Pot roast, the regular way this time, and then pie. I ate the crust. He pretended to be upset about it.

On the couch he kissed me, and nothing moved. Not one thing. I laughed right into it with my mouth wide open.

He pulled back an inch, same as before, and I braced for it.

He said, "You okay?"

I said, "Walt, I'm better than okay."

That's all you're getting out of me about that Saturday.

But I'll tell you about last month. Walt took me to a cabin on the lake for two whole nights.

When I packed, I stood in my bathroom out of habit. I was looking for the cream, the travel brush, the little mirror I used to check my plate in.

Then I zipped the bag. I didn't pack one single thing for my teeth.

I did Zyara Friday morning at my sink and honestly didn't think about my mouth again until Sunday night.

We ate ribs off the grill on the dock, and I gnawed them right down to the bone. I had a caramel apple from the bait shop. We split a bag of popcorn by the fire, and not one hull got under my plate. My coffee on the porch tasted like coffee.

For the first time in six years I went away for a weekend without worrying my teeth would slip. And I didn't bring a thing for them.
