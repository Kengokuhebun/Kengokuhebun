My sister and I both came off GLP-1 in March. She kept it off. I gained back 22 pounds by June. Now I know why it happened to me and not her.

My sister Linda and I started semaglutide the same week last fall, through the same insurance plan, because we work for the same school district and it covered the shots that year.

Same medication. Same dose. Shots on Sunday nights, weigh ins on Friday mornings, a text thread called Shrinking Sisters.

I’m 56. She’s 53. Same mother, same build, same habit of eating standing up at the kitchen counter.

And for eight months, we got the same result. She lost 44. I lost 41. We bought dresses for our niece’s wedding in sizes neither of us had seen in twenty years.

Then in March, the district changed plans. The new one didn’t cover the shots. $1,100 a month cash. Neither of us could afford that.

So we came off. Same month. Same last shot, more or less. We joked that at least we’d be miserable together.

Four months later, Linda was up 3 pounds.

I was up 22.

Same drug. Same eight months. Same last Sunday. And when I finally found out why, it had NOTHING to do with the medication leaving my system, my habits, or how badly I wanted it.

Let me back up, because I know some of you are watching your own number climb right now, and I know exactly what people are saying to you.

The first two weeks off, I was fine. I want to say that, because it’s the part that made what came next so confusing.

Then, around week three, the food noise came back. Not slowly. Like someone switched a radio on. That constant chatter about what to eat next, which I hadn’t heard in eight months. Standing in the pantry at 9pm having a full conversation with a box of crackers.

I expected some of that. Everybody had warned me. What I didn’t expect was the pattern.

Calm week, the noise was there but I could talk over it. Scale held, or up half a pound.

Hard week, and I was having a lot of them, my daughter’s wedding, my hours cut, my mother’s hip, the noise took the whole house. Scale up two pounds by Friday, sometimes three. Like stress had a manual override on everything I’d learned in eight months.

Linda’s texts every Friday: same number. Same number. Up one. Same number.

I posted in my group. And I got forty answers, I’m sure you can imagine.

“That’s just what happens. Everyone regains. The drug was doing the work.”

“You should have tapered.” Nobody told me to taper.

“You didn’t build the habits while you were on it.” I built every habit. I’d weighed a tablespoon of peanut butter for eight months.

“It’s like blood pressure medication. You don’t just stop.” I didn’t choose to stop. My plan chose.

“You need to get back on, whatever it costs.” With what?

And then the one I still think about.

“Honestly, the shots were never going to fix your relationship with food. That part was always going to be on you.”

I read that in my car outside the school, between the morning bell and my next shift. I cried where nobody could see me.

Because here’s the thing about regaining. For eight months everyone told me the drug was doing the work, not me. Then I came off and regained, and the same people told me the regain was me. The drug got the credit and I got the blame. Both ways. Convenient.

So I went to my doctor.

She ran labs, because I asked her to. Thyroid, full panel, the works. Everything came back fine.

And then she said the sentence I’ve been chewing on ever since.

“Marie, this is expected. The medication was managing the appetite. Without it, the weight returns. When you can afford to, we’ll restart.”

Expected.

So now I had a name. Rebound. Expected rebound.

Do you know how that lands? I’d spent my whole life being told it was willpower. Then a drug came along and proved it was noise, not willpower, and for eight months I finally got to be the person I always suspected I could be. And then it was taken away, for a reason that had nothing to do with me, and I was handed the old diagnosis back within a month.

And my sister, who took the exact same drug for the exact same eight months and came off the exact same week, was up 3 pounds and buying sandals.

And here’s the part nobody could explain.

My sleep, which had been bad on the shots, was worse off them. Wired at 3am, heart doing something I could feel, wide awake over nothing. My watch kept flagging it. Elevated heart rate, sleep score in the gutter. My body was screaming that something was wrong while every blood test said I was fine.

Meanwhile my group was two highlight reels. The women still on, down 60, down 80, goal weight. And the women who’d come off, posting their regain like a confession, and me nodding along, because that’s what happens.

One night at 1am I googled “regain after stopping semaglutide.”

And there it was. Articles about the trials. Most people regain most of it within a year. Charts. I read it with my heart pounding and thought, that’s me. I’m the chart. I’m the proof.

I drafted a message to my doctor. “Just put me back on. I’ll figure out the money.” I had no way to figure out the money. It sat in my phone. Unsent.

Then I asked my sister the question that changed everything.

Saturday, her kitchen, the two of us folding wedding programs. She was in a top I’d watched her buy in February. It still fit.

“I need the truth. What are you doing that I’m not?”

She put down the programs. “You’re going to be mad.”

“Why would I be mad?”

“Because it’s something my doctor made me start before my first shot. And made me keep taking after my last one. It costs about $30. And I assumed yours had you on it too, because mine acted like it was standard.”

Before Linda’s first injection, her functional medicine doctor told her something Linda wrote down in her phone, word for word.

She slid it across the table.

“Every woman I put on these medications starts magnesium first. Because the shot is about to create the exact deficiency that stops it from working. And if she ever comes off, that deficiency is the only thing left running the show.”

I read it twice. Then I looked up at her.

Magnesium?

I TOOK magnesium. Every night, the whole eight months. For, let’s say, the reason every woman on these shots ends up taking it. And then I stopped it when I stopped the shots, because I thought it was a shot thing.

“What kind?”

The drugstore kind. I had no idea.

“That’s the problem. Wrong form. And you stopped it the exact week you needed it most. Now let her explain it the way she explained it to me. Because it explains your March, and my March.”

So here it is, the way Linda gave it to me.

Your stress response works like a thermostat. Turns up under pressure, turns back down after. Magnesium is the fuel that lets it turn back down. When you’re depleted, the thermostat sticks on high, and your body reads that as an emergency.

An emergency means storage mode. Hold onto everything. Release nothing.

The shot quiets your appetite. It does nothing about storage mode. So you can take your foot off the gas all you want. Stress is still standing on the brake.

“While you were on the shot,” Linda said, “your foot was off the gas so hard it didn’t much matter what the brake was doing. You still lost. We both did.”

Now here’s the trap, and nobody had ever once walked me through it.

Almost all of your magnesium comes from food. Most women over 50 are already running low. Stress burns it, medications drain it, even the soil our food grows in has less than it used to.

Then you start a GLP-1, and suddenly you’re eating half of what you ate before. The drug is doing its job perfectly.

But half the food means half the magnesium.

“You weren’t empty on day one,” Linda said. “You’d been eating normally your whole life. Then for eight months you ate half as much. Every week there was less coming in. The longer you were on, the emptier the tank on the day you came off.”

“Then the shot leaves. Your appetite comes back, which is normal, that happens to everyone. Foot back on the gas.”

“But your thermostat is stuck on high with nothing to turn it down. Storage mode. And now storage mode has a full appetite to work with. That’s your 22 pounds. That’s why it’s week three, not day one. That’s why hard weeks are two pounds and calm weeks are half.”

“I came off with a full tank, because she never let me run it down. My appetite came back too. But after a hard day, my thermostat turned back down. My body wasn’t in an emergency. It just went back to eating normally and stayed roughly where it was.”

And then she said the thing that shut me up.

“Marie, if regaining is just what the drug does, why did the drug do it to you and not to me?”

I pushed back. Obviously.

Everything they tested was fine. The thing that wouldn’t have been fine, nobody tested. They had a word for it instead. Expected. And a word is where the investigating stops.
