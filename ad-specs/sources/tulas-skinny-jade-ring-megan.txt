For two years, I didn't take this ring off once. Then a woman in navy scrubs held out a little plastic cup and said, "All jewelry in here, doc. That one too."

It was 7:15 on a Thursday morning in the imaging wing of our hospital. But instead of my white coat, I was in a gown and grippy socks, about to spend forty minutes inside an MRI machine for a shoulder I'd ignored for a year.

And I stood there with my hand cupped over my ring like she'd asked for my credit cards.

I've walked patients through this exact moment more times than I can count. But my heart was going like I'd just sprinted up four flights of stairs.

Over a ring.

I'll tell you what happened inside that machine. But first you need to know that two years earlier, I was the last person on earth who would have cared about this.

My name is Megan. I'm 34, and I'm a family doctor. I wash my hands about eighty times a day and pull on gloves about forty more.

Rings and I have never gotten along. Under gloves, they tear the nitrile. At the sink, they trap soap. In 2019, the little diamond in my engagement ring went down a clinic drain, and we never found it.

After that, my jewelry lived in the cup holder of my Honda. Wedding band included. I pulled them off in the parking lot before clinic, and half the time they never made it back on.

My husband Dev used to fish them out from under the parking stubs. I honestly didn't mind. A bare hand was just what a doctor's hand looked like.

That changed because of a woman named Joy.

I swim laps at the Y at 5:30 every morning before clinic. Joy is 66, and she has owned lane one since before I could drive. Every morning when I got in, she was already halfway through her session.

And on her left wrist, every single morning, was a jade bangle. Pale green, worn smooth, going in and out of the water with every stroke.

One morning while she was toweling off, I finally asked. "Do you ever take that thing off?"

She laughed at me. "Not since 1991. My mother put it on me with dish soap the week I got married."

"Not even here?" I pointed at the pool.

"Especially in here. Thirty-some years of chlorine, honey. It looks better than I do."

Then she told me the part I didn't expect.

In her family, jade isn't something you change with your outfit. You put it on once and you leave it on. Her grandmother believed jade slowly gets to know the person wearing it. And if it ever breaks, it took a hit that was meant for you.

I laughed. "Joy, I'm a doctor. Every month a patient asks me if jade will lower their blood pressure, and every month I tell them no."

She shrugged and tucked her towel under her arm.

"Believe whatever you want," she said. "Just leave it on and see."

That line followed me around for weeks.

Because it was a hard spring. My mom was in and out of appointments nobody could explain to us, not even to me. Dev's hours got cut. I was covering two clinics across town and eating lunch in the car, next to a cup holder full of rings and bracelets.

I zone out on my lunch break with whatever my phone feeds me. That week, after one question at the pool, it fed me jade.

A grandmother in Taipei working a bangle onto her granddaughter's wrist with lotion, telling her to leave it on until it breaks. A woman holding up a bangle she'd worn for forty years, the green twice as deep as it was in her wedding photos. And under every video, hundreds of women who hadn't taken theirs off in decades, a few of them swearing their luck turned the year they put it on.

I rolled my eyes. I'm a doctor. I know exactly what an algorithm does after you ask one question.

And yet I watched every single one, in the car, while my burrito went cold. And that night I kept picturing Joy's wrist going in and out of the water, thirty-some years and never once off.

I didn't want magic. I wanted one thing that didn't change every time I got home from a long shift.

Something constant.

But there was one more thing stopping me.

I'm not Asian.

I asked Joy, a little embarrassed, if it would be weird for me to wear jade.

She actually snorted. "Jade doesn't check your passport. My granddaughter has her entire dorm wearing it. If you respect it, you can have it."

So I went looking. A bangle felt like a tattoo. I wanted something more subtle.

But I got a shock. This is where it got expensive.

The first one came from a kiosk at the outlet mall. Sixty-four dollars. A thick, rounded band of green stone that looked gorgeous on its little velvet stand.

On my hand, it was a disaster. It pushed my fingers apart. It ripped through two pairs of gloves before lunch. And the first night I slept in it, I woke up with a red dent in the next finger over.

So it came off at night. Then it came off for clinic. By the end of the month, it was in the cup holder with everything else.

That's the problem nobody warns you about. Thick rings are made to look good in a photo. A ring you can't live in always ends up off your hand.

So I went the other way.

Nine dollars online. "Natural jadeite," with four thousand sold.

It weighed about as much as a Cheerio. By the time I had it over my knuckle, it was already the same temperature as my skin.

And the green was perfect. A little too perfect…

One flat, glassy shade all the way around, like a gummy bear.

After a week of hand sanitizer, it was cloudy and scratched.

That night I did what I always do when I feel dumb. I went on Reddit and read until 1 a.m.

There are whole communities of people who wear jade the way Joy does.

One wrote, "I wear a jade bangle that I can no longer remove. I've had it on for 12 years. I wear it swimming, camping, doing yard work, everything."

When a nervous new owner asked if summer swimming would ruin hers, someone answered, "Mine's been on since about 2002. It's fine. You're overthinking."

Then I found the scary threads.

Someone who paid thirteen hundred Canadian dollars for a bangle with a certificate, and was now "thinking I got scammed."

People posting their "jade" and hearing things like, "it could be glass. Definitely not jadeite."

I did the math on my own. Seventy-three dollars spent, and my hand was still bare.

But one comment changed how I saw all of it. It had 146 upvotes:

"If it is very cheap but super beautiful, it is usually possible that it has been processed or there is something concealed."

Read that again. My nine-dollar ring had been too beautiful. That was the giveaway.

Real jade is a stone that came out of the ground. At an honest price, it's a little cloudy, with veins and uneven patches of color. As one jade fan put it, "jade's natural so it won't be perfectly uniform."

The flaws are how you know.

I told Joy all of this the next morning at the hairdryers. She just held out her wet wrist. "Touch it."

Her bangle was cold. Cold like a stethoscope in January. And when I kept my fingers on it, it warmed up so slowly.

"Cold first, then warm," she said. "Glass warms up in a second. Plastic is never cold at all."

Then she turned her wrist toward the window. A darker vein ran through one side, next to a cloudy patch near the edge. "No two pieces match. If every ring in the picture looks identical, keep scrolling."

"So where does your granddaughter get hers?" I asked.

She didn't even have to think. "Tulas. I checked hers myself before I let her wear it."

Which brings me back to that plastic cup.

My shoulder had been clicking for a year. Twenty years of freestyle will do that. I've ordered more MRIs than I can count, and I'd never once been on the table. It was routine, and I'm completely fine. This isn't a medical story.

But MRI rooms have one rule. Nothing on your body but the gown.

My earrings went in first. My wedding band slid right off. Then I got to the jade, and I just stopped.

I had to work it over my knuckle. Two years had made it snug.

When it finally came off, there it was. A stripe of pale skin where the ring had been, smooth and a little shiny, with a faint dent pressed into it. Two summers of weekend lake swims, and that one band of my finger had never seen the sun.

My hand looked like a stranger's.

The tech dropped my ring in the cup, snapped on the lid, and locked it in locker 9.

Then I lay in that tube for forty minutes with a plastic cage over my face and a voice in my headphones telling me not to move.

My thumb would not stop looking for it. Over and over, it reached for the ring and found bare skin, and every time my stomach dropped. Where is it? Then I'd remember. Locker 9.

I must have done it a dozen times.

I found out later I'm not the only one. Someone on Reddit wrote, "I can't bear losing a jewellery item that I wear, it feels like you're naked."

That was it exactly. My hand felt naked.

When I came out, the nurse who'd held the plastic cup caught me rubbing the pale stripe with my thumb.

"First time off?" she asked.

Then she pushed up her sleeve. A jade bangle, dark green, with one tiny chip near the edge.

"First time in two years," I replied.

She nodded like that was the most obvious thing in the world. "That chip is from 2008. I fell outside the post office on Christmas Eve and landed right on this wrist." She tapped it. "My mother said, 'There. It took that one for you.'"

As a doctor, I can tell you exactly what happened to that wrist. As a woman sitting there with a bare hand, I liked her mother's version better.

Then she patted my hand. "Go get it out of the locker, honey."

I did. I nearly dropped the key.

I held the ring in my palm first. Cold. Then slowly warm. Like it was deciding all over again.

I slid it back over my knuckle and onto that pale stripe of skin, and I let out a breath I'd been holding since 7:15 that morning.
