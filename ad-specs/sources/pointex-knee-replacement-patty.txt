I've had two knee replacements. The first recovery almost BROKE me. The second one, I was sleeping through the night by week three.

If you had yours in the last six months, please read this.

Not because it'll make you feel better. Because what you do in these first few months decides how that knee ends up, and nobody told me that the first time.

My first knee, I did everything right. The ice machine. The pills right on the clock. PT three times a week. And I still spent four months sleeping two hours at a time in a recliner, gritting my teeth through every PT session, and terrified they'd put me back under to force my knee to bend.

My second knee was the same surgeon. Same hospital. Same PT.

But it didn't hurt anything like the first one. My first knee never dropped below an 8. My second one sat at a 3. Some days, a 1.

The surgery didn't change. What changed was something around the implant that no X-ray shows.

Nobody mentioned it the first time. Not my surgeon. Not my PT. Not the thick packet they sent me home with.

It took a physical therapist with over 50 years in practice and a head full of gray hair to finally tell me what actually makes a knee replacement recovery faster.

Let me back up.

My name is Patty. I'm 66. I had my left knee replaced three years ago, in March.

The first three weeks were total hell fire. I'm not going to sugarcoat it.

I couldn't sleep in my own bed.

Every time I rolled over, the pain woke me up.

No matter where I put my leg, it hurt, so I slept in the recliner for four months.

I taught myself to wake up just enough to flip my leg over, then try to fall back asleep. Two hours at a time. Every night.

My husband Ron would find me in that chair at six in the morning, wide awake, crying.

And PT was its own nightmare. I did every exercise they gave me. Twice a day at home, three times a week at the clinic. And I hated it, because it hurt so much afterwards I could barely get back in the car.

At week eight, my PT measured my bend. 95 degrees.

She looked at the number and got quiet. Then she said, "If we don't get past this soon, your surgeon might want to do a manipulation."

That's where they put you back under and force your knee to bend.

I went home and didn't sleep at all that night. My anxiety was through the roof.

I did get past it, eventually. Barely. But that knee never felt right. Three years later, it still ached every time it rained, and I still couldn't kneel on it.

So when my surgeon said my right knee was ready to go last spring, I told him the same thing a lot of us tell our surgeons.

"I just can't do this again."

He talked me into it anyway. And a week before the surgery, they sent me to a different PT for prehab.

Her name was Marian. Seventy-four years old. Fifty-one years in practice. Gray hair pulled back in a clip, reading glasses on a chain, and hands like a farmer.

She asked me how my first recovery went.

I told her everything. The recliner. The two hours at a time. The 95 degrees. The word "manipulation."

She just nodded the whole time, like she'd heard it a thousand times.

"The worst part was the bending, wasn't it?" she said.

"Every single time."

"And everyone told you it was just the knee healing."

"Isn't it?"

"No, honey," Marian said. "It wasn't the knee. It wasn't the metal. And it wasn't you."

I just looked at her.

"Your cartilage used to cushion the knee," she said. "It also protected thousands of tiny nerve endings around it."

"Okay."

"To put the new knee in, the surgeon takes the cartilage out and puts metal in its place. Right after surgery, those nerve endings are raw against the metal. And they keep firing."

"So that's the pain?"

"That's the pain. In most people, they settle down over time. In some, they never do. And here's what nobody tells you." She leaned in. "The longer they keep firing, the more stuck they get."

I didn't say anything. I was thinking about the recliner.

"Think about what happens," she said. "Every time you bend, those nerves scream. So you hold back. You do fewer reps. You don't push the bend. You can't sleep, so you're exhausted. And a knee you don't bend gets stiff. And a stiff knee hurts even more."

"So it just keeps going."

"Round and round," Marian said. "That's why your first recovery took forever. You weren't doing anything wrong. You were fighting nerves the whole time, and nobody gave you anything for them."

"Then why didn't anyone see it? My X-rays were always perfect."

"Because X-rays show bone and metal," she said. "Not nerves."

Three years. I'd thought I was just weak.

"So what do I do differently this time?" I asked.

"You calm those nerves down, as early as you can. It's called Counter-Frequency Therapy."

"What does that mean?"
